The craft

Nothing here is
glued shut.

Four joints, three species, five benches. This page is the whole method, including the parts that make our furniture slower and more expensive than it needs to be.

One — the yard

We buy standing timber and then wait.

Kiln-dried stock is cheaper, faster, and behaves worse. Forcing moisture out in six days leaves stresses in the board that show up two winters later as a cupped tabletop. So our timber goes onto sticks in the yard behind the mill and stays there — twenty months minimum for ash, closer to thirty for thick oak.

It means we are always building from wood we bought three years ago, and it means a bad guess about demand is expensive. We are currently working through boards purchased in 2021 from a coppiced woodland in Perthshire that has been cut on a fifteen-year rotation since the 1870s.

Two — the joinery

Four joints do almost all of the work.

Drawbored mortise-and-tenon

Mora trestles, Quarry posts

The pin hole through the tenon is offset by 1.5 mm. Driving a riven oak pin bends it, and the bend pulls the shoulder permanently closed. It cannot back out, and no glue is doing the work.

Wedged through-tenon

Haldane arms, Mora chairs

The tenon passes right through and is split with two wedges from the outside. Tightening it later takes a mallet and ten seconds. You can see the end grain, which is the point.

Breadboard end

Mora tops, Quarry desk

A cross-grain end cap pinned through elongated holes. The top can move about 4 mm across a heating season instead of splitting itself trying not to.

Webbed slat cradle

Linden bed

Slats sit in stitched cotton webbing rather than a steel channel. Removes the tick of timber on metal and the creak of a slat rolling in a loose seat. Both, entirely.

Three — the benches

One maker builds the whole piece.

No stations, no handovers. Whoever chalks the boards cuts the joints, fits the drawers, and signs the inside of the frame in pencil.

Four — the finish

Four options, all of them repairable in a room.

Soap

Flakes dissolved in hot water, brushed on, dried, repeated. Matte, almost chalky, and the only finish you can repair with a cloth and a kettle.

Hard wax oil

Warms the timber and shrugs off a wine glass. Two coats, cut back between. Re-oil a worn edge without stripping the piece.

Lye + white oil

Lye kills the yellowing in ash, then white pigmented oil holds it pale. The Scandinavian answer to a dark winter room.

Fumed

Ammonia vapour reacting with tannin in oak. Deep brown, right through the surface rather than sat on top. Oak only, and no two batches match exactly.
